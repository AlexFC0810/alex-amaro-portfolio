Operating leverage

One operator, few handoffs.

A growth problem travels a long chain — signal, research, the economics, the decision, the system, the build, the measurement — and normally it changes hands four or five times on the way. Here it mostly does not, and that is the thing AI actually changed about this job. Below is where that shows: client systems first, then my own. Each caption says what you will really find, including where you will hit a sign-in wall. Client systems

Agentic research

Autonomous Opportunity Radar

TypeScript, on a 30-minute GitHub Actions cron. It has no public URL because it holds live inbox and Slack credentials, so instead of a screenshot, here is the pipeline:

saved-search email
   ↓ IMAP, skip anything tagged $RadarDone
parse  → title · URL · budget
   ↓
score  → apply / screen / skip   (the rubric is the IP)
   ↓
draft  → Claude, prompt-cached templates
   ↓
post   → Slack, proposal attached
   ↓
mark   → set $RadarDone   (dedup survives a stateless cron)

The judgment call is the interesting part. Version one read the official API — clean, but the key sits behind a manual approval gate that can stall for months, and once did. A perfect input that never turns on is worth zero. So the input became the inbox instead: no approval needed, unambiguously within terms, and that single change also deleted the dedup database, the bot and the always-on server. The workflow also ships dormant-safe — with no secrets set it exits green with a notice rather than failing every 30 minutes, so it arms itself the day the credentials land.

It reports into a single-operator dashboard I built for myself — a daily send floor, one next action, and a copyable go-live command instead of twelve charts.

The craft

One offer, eight hooks.

Media buying is mostly a question of how many honest ways you can say the same thing before the market tells you which one it wanted. This is one chiropractic screening offer written eight ways — same price, same proof, eight different doors in. The variable under test is where on the awareness ladder the reader is standing when the ad reaches them, and one of the eight is the control: no emotional hook at all, just the offer.

The numbers

Fewer, and sourced.

Cost per lead is quoted per campaign, never blended across an account set — a blended figure mixes brand spend with lead-gen spend and means nothing. Every line here traces to a specific account, and each one carries the boundary it will not cross. The head-to-head figure at the top of this page is the seventh; it is not repeated here.

$225K+

Meta ad spend managed across 14+ accounts in the U.S., Canada and the U.K.

Medspa, aesthetics, chiropractic, tattoo removal, weight loss, local service and B2B. Managed and supported — a large share of it on awareness objectives, which is exactly why it must never be divided by a lead count.

~US$5.40

Average cost per lead across an entire Canadian body-contouring account — 1,192 leads at CA$7.39, with 80 booked appointments tracked.

CA$8,804.13 across 13 lead campaigns of 15. The account average is honest because the whole account was lead-objective; the currency travels with the number, because quoting the local figure unmarked would read as roughly 27% more expensive than it was.

730+

Tattoo-removal leads at ~$19–$21 cost per lead, in a saturated Brooklyn and New York City market.

Flagship campaign 526 leads at $19 — the account total is never quoted at the flagship’s price. My longest-running client, roughly two years and still running, and the one account where the client went on camera.

70

Appointments booked in a 30-day window for a weight-loss clinic opening.

61 of them fell inside the calendar month. Booked means scheduled, not attended.

$1.88

Cost per lead across 817 B2B leads for a U.K. commercial-refrigeration installer.

The flagship campaign ran 709 of them at about $1.50. B2B at consumer-grade cost — the cheap exception, not the rule, and it is labelled as one everywhere it appears.

6,250+

Qualified leads across medspa, aesthetics, chiropractic, tattoo-removal, weight-loss and B2B campaigns.

Qualified is doing real work in that sentence: the consumer forms carried qualifying questions and the B2B forms filtered for $20K+/month businesses. Sales-ready, not raw volume.

The record

Every number here stands cross-examination.

A marketer’s portfolio has one structural weakness: the figures are assertions, and the reader has no way to test them. This one answers that mechanically rather than rhetorically. The contract is published and machine-readable, the adversarial audit is public with the attacker’s prompt included, an agent will answer hostile questions from the contract alone, and the repository this page is served from refuses to publish a figure the contract does not hold.

The guard on this repository

The source of this page is public, and every push runs a salted-hash leak tripwire and a contract-integrity check. The eighteen case pages are not hand-written — they are rendered from the contract by a build that refuses to emit any figure it cannot find there, so a case with no approved claim dies on its first digit. That is the AI-native part I would actually defend: not that a model wrote something, but that a gate I built runs on every commit whether or not I am paying attention.

What this page does not claim

  • Not client revenue, and not ROAS. I do not have post-booking visibility, and closing is the client’s side of the line. What I claim is leads, booked appointments and the follow-up systems between them.
  • Not a blended cost per lead. Total spend divided by total leads is not a CPL and I will not quote one.
  • Not scaled AI voice. Shipped, and ran, from February 2025 — at single-digit to low-double enrolment volumes.
  • Not client identities. Clients appear by vertical and country only.
  • Not a portfolio-wide appointment total. Bookings are claimed per client, tied to the campaigns that produced them, because a cross-client total would count appointments my campaigns did not generate.
