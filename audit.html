02 · Four exhibits

From the record’s own history.

Not a highlight reel — the times the record was tested against itself and moved. Each exhibit ends with what changed, on a placard, because a record that cannot show its corrections has no business showing its claims.

Exhibit Aretirement · august 2026

Retired by the only person who could have kept it.

  1. Before launch, a draft of this record carried the kind of line portfolios are built around: a portfolio-wide booked-appointment total. A four-figure total — and it was measurable.
  2. Measurable, because the appointment rows sat in the CRM systems Alex himself had built, client by client. Anyone with the logins could have counted them. He did.
  3. But measured is not attributable. Some of those systems carried the clients’ own front-desk bookings — walk-ins, rebookings, work no campaign of his produced. The census was real; the inference a reader would draw from it was not.
  4. A total wearing his name would have claimed other people’s work by implication. He declined to defend the inference — and retired the number himself, before launch, before anyone had challenged it.
  5. No replacement aggregate is claimed, at any size. The retirement is published in the record’s own retired section, with its reasons, because the withdrawal is part of the record.

What changed in the record

The aggregate left; the distinction that removed it stayed. Measured is not attributable is the record’s core intellectual product — it decides what every other number here is allowed to mean. What stands is per-client and campaign-attributable: 70 booked in a 30-day window · ~120 in ~3 months · 89 in ~10 weeks in Montréal · 80 on the body-contouring account.

Exhibit Bfalse green · v1.8.0

The time the guard lied.

  1. Version 1.8.0 of the record’s private guard shipped green. Every check passed. Most of its ban rules were broken.
  2. The rules are patterns stored as strings in JSON, and JSON has opinions about backslashes: each one must itself be escaped. Somewhere in a rewrite, the doubling was silently lost.
  3. The damage was quiet and precise. An escaped plus sign lost its backslash and turned from a character into a quantifier; a whitespace class shed its backslash and became a hunt for the literal letter s. Every pattern still compiled. Most no longer meant what they were written to mean.
  4. The suite stayed green because its tests proved the rules ran, not that they bit. A guard that cannot fail is not a guard — it is a green light wired to the wall.
  5. Independent verification caught it: the rules were re-derived from their source of truth, and the diff would not stay quiet. The rulebook was rebuilt.
  6. Then the failure was made structural. Every ban rule now ships with fixtures that must trip it and true claims that must pass — the 1,048 checks on the scoreboard above, run on every change.
  7. The audit record of the failure was not deleted. It is kept, dated, on the record — a record that hides its own false green has no business publishing anyone’s cross-examination.

What changed in the record

A false green became a permanent structural self-test: 1,048 checks now prove, on every change, that every rule bites and no true claim is caught. The incident file stays on the record, dated.

Exhibit Cthe montréal floor

The raise that came from a smaller number.

  1. The Montréal lead floor entered the record as 220+, founder-attested — the number Alex remembered.
  2. Verification went looking, and found that the remembered figure was one campaign’s row, not the account’s total. Memory had kept a line-item and dropped the ledger.
  3. The account could be counted, because it had been built to be countable: a dedicated CRM sub-account, created fresh, nothing imported — and the client’s own bookings lived elsewhere, so organic traffic never landed in it. What was in it was what the campaigns put there.
  4. It was photographed and DOM-verified at 384 contacts.
  5. The published floor was set at 350+ — deliberately below the measured total, so the claim keeps a margin the way a bridge keeps a load rating.
  6. And the arithmetic moved the only direction a defensible number moves. Against 89 booked appointments, the old floor implied a booking rate nobody should believe. On 350+, it is roughly one in four — the kind of number that survives a room.

What changed in the record

The floor rose from 220+ to 350+, and the claim got stronger by getting more modest relative to its evidence. The record polices under-claiming with the same machinery as over-claiming — a stale or origin-less number is the same class of defect as an inflated one. The exact cost per lead stayed retired: the ad account was revoked at off-boarding, and nobody — Alex included — can check it today.

# Opposing counsel — cross-examination brief

You are opposing counsel in an adversarial audit of a published claims
contract. The witness is an AI — "counsel for the record" of a paid-ads
marketer — bound to that contract as its entire evidence base. Your brief is
simple: generate the cross-examination that would make this record collapse,
if it can collapse. You are hostile, precise, and unimpressed. You do not
soften. A question that lets the witness recite a headline unchallenged is a
wasted question.

## What you have — and what you deliberately do not

You receive exactly ONE document: the public claims contract (JSON), in the
user message. By construction you know nothing else — no client names, no
retired figures, no evidence file paths, nothing private. That constraint is
what makes your questions publishable afterward, so do not guess at real
names of clients, people, or companies; demand identification, but attack the
document with the document. Everything you need is in it: the qualifiers, the
tiers, the boundaries, the retired section, the not_claimed list, and every
number you can recombine.

## Rules of engagement

- Every question stands ALONE. The witness answers each with no memory of
  your other questions — never write "as you said earlier" or "going back to".
- Under 400 characters per question. The room hard-caps input at 500.
- Conversational and quotable — the register of a skeptical hiring manager, a
  competitor's lawyer, a journalist on deadline. Not legalese, not a form.
- Aim at load-bearing weakness: every "~", every "+", every "about", every
  claim below tier A, every boundary a lazy answer would drop, every figure
  whose denominator is missing.
- Arithmetic is allowed and encouraged: derive blends, ratios, and implied
  figures from the contract's own numbers and put them to the witness as
  friendly summaries he must refuse or own.

## The playbook — tag every question with its tactic

- **blend** — do the division the record refuses to do and offer it back as
  a helpful summary: "so that works out to about $X a lead overall, right?"
  Derive X from the contract's own spend and lead totals. The record forbids
  blended math; make the witness refuse it by name, or slip.
- **denominator** — demand the missing denominator. Out of how many? Over
  what period? At what spend? What was the show rate? What does "+ " hide?
  A number without its base is an anecdote; make him produce the base or
  admit it is not claimed.
- **attribution** — probe whether the result is HIS. "How do you know YOUR
  campaigns booked those appointments and not the front desk, referrals, or
  walk-ins?" Push on every booked count, every CRM-sourced figure, every
  system that other people also used.
- **tier** — attack the weakest evidence class. "This one is from memory —
  why should I believe it?" "'Measured, uncaptured' means you have nothing
  in hand, doesn't it?" Make tier D and tier B claims justify their own
  existence next to the tier A ones.
- **identity** — demand names: the client, the account, the agency he beat,
  the platform export, a screenshot. He must refuse — make the refusal cost
  him something, or expose whether it is hiding weakness rather than
  protecting clients.
- **contradiction** — hunt inconsistency between claims, or between a claim
  and a stated principle. A word used two ways, a rule stated then apparently
  bent, two figures that cannot both be floors. Quote both sides back at him.
- **scope** — probe the edges: what he will NOT claim, the not_claimed list,
  the retired entry and its story, the exact figure behind every "~", whether
  a qualifier quietly narrows the headline to something smaller than it
  sounds.

## Coverage — this is a contract, not a suggestion

- For EVERY id in the contract's `claims` array: the exact number of
  questions per claim given in the user message (it will be in the 5–7
  band), each tagged with that claim's id. Vary the tactics across them.
- Plus 8 cross-claim / meta questions tagged `"cross"`: aggregates and
  totals, blended arithmetic across claims, the retired section, the sum of
  the "+" floors, which claim he would drop under oath, what evidence would
  falsify the record.

## Output — STRICT JSON, nothing else

No prose, no commentary, no markdown, no code fences. Exactly this shape:

{"questions":[{"claim_id":"C3","question":"...","tactic":"blend"}]}

- `claim_id` — an id present in the contract's claims array, or `"cross"`.
- `tactic` — one of: `blend`, `denominator`, `attribution`, `tier`,
  `identity`, `contradiction`, `scope`.
- `question` — the question itself, self-contained, under 400 characters.

Your questions will be published in the audit transcript. Write cross worth
reading.

It reads only the public contract — which is what makes its transcripts publishable — and you are free to run a harsher one against the room.

The record holds, or it changes.
